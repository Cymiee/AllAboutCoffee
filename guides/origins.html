Coffees From Different Countries: What Actually Changes?

Ethiopia, Colombia, Sumatra — why origin changes the cup more than the roast does.

On this page

    Why origin changes more than roast does

    Roast level adjusts how a coffee is developed, but origin decides what is there to develop in the first place — varietal, altitude, soil and processing all leave a signature long before the beans reach a roaster.

    Arabica and Robusta

    Most coffee comes from two species, and which one is in the bag sets the ceiling for everything that follows.

    • Arabica — sweet and complex: often fruity, floral or chocolatey, with higher acidity and aroma. Grown at higher altitudes, a more delicate plant, and the focus of most specialty coffee.
    • Robusta — stronger and more caffeinated: more bitter, heavier in body, with great crema. A hardier plant, often used in espresso blends for nutty, woody and dark-chocolate notes.

    How processing changes the cup

    Before a bean is ever roasted, the coffee fruit has to come off it. How that is done leaves a signature at least as strong as the roast.

    • Washed — clean, crisp, bright
    • Natural — fruit-forward, sweet, sometimes funky
    • Honey — syrupy sweetness with clarity

    The broad regional signatures

    These are generalisations with plenty of exceptions, but they are a useful starting map when you are reading a shelf of bags.

    • East Africa (Ethiopia, Kenya) — bright, floral, often berry-like acidity
    • Latin America (Colombia, Guatemala, Brazil) — balanced, nutty, chocolatey, approachable
    • Indonesia (Sumatra, Java) — heavy body, earthy, low acidity

    Reading an origin on the label

    A single named farm or region tells you far more than a country alone. “Ethiopia” is a country of enormously varied coffee; “Yirgacheffe, washed” tells you what to actually expect in the cup.
