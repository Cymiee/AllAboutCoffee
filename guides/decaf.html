Decaf, Explained

How the caffeine comes out, whether it hurts the flavour, and what to look for on the bag.

On this page

    How the caffeine comes out

    Caffeine is removed from green, unroasted beans, using water, solvents or CO₂. The method is usually stated on the bag, and it is the single biggest predictor of whether a decaf tastes good.

    • Swiss Water — solvent-free, uses only water and filtration
    • CO₂ process — gentle, well regarded, typically pricier
    • Solvent-based — most common and cheapest; quality varies widely

    Does it wreck the flavour?

    It used to, and that reputation has outlived the reality. Decaffeination does strip some aromatic compounds, but a carefully processed, freshly roasted decaf is now a genuinely good cup — the bad reputation mostly comes from stale supermarket decaf rather than the process itself.

    What to look for

    A stated process, a roast date, and a named origin. Decaf is more fragile than regular coffee, so freshness matters even more than usual.
